Ontario's transport gap

Ontario has a patient transportation industry. It does not have a patient transportation system. Five separate networks move the same patients on different days of the week, and none of them can see the others.

01

What the survey found

Six findings drive every recommendation that follows. Each is sourced; each carries a confidence rating in the accompanying register.

Finding 01

The waste is documented and large

The only population-level study of Ontario inter-facility transfers found ~400,000 a year costing ~$283M, with 80% routine and non-urgent — largely carried by fully-equipped 911 ambulances. The most common trip: 10.5 km to a cardiologist or a dialysis chair.

Finding 02

There is no payer

OHIP does not cover non-emergency transport. Emergency ambulance carries a regulated $45 co-payment; non-urgent stretcher transport is unregulated in price and uncovered, with no exemption available even on social assistance.

Finding 03

The US playbook does not transfer

American NEMT software is built on Medicaid managed-care brokers that supply volume, prior authorization and a claims rail. Ontario has neither a broker layer nor a claims rail. Importing that architecture builds a product with nothing to plug into.

Finding 04

The buyer is fragmented — but real

Hospitals, LTC operators, community agencies and regional renal programs each hold a piece. Two are single-payer pools: NIHB medical transportation in Ontario at $166.0M and growing ~20% a year, and the Northern Health Travel Grant.

Finding 05

Supply is consolidating

Transdev/Voyago — 600+ medical attendants, positioned as Canada's largest NEPT brand — has been acquiring regional operators. A neutral coordination layer gets harder to build every year consolidation runs.

Finding 06

A vendor just collapsed under this market

Quebec ePCR vendor Prehos gave notice on 8 June 2026 and ceased operating 7 July, owing ~$10M. ~22 Ontario paramedic services were displaced on under a month's notice; some reverted to paper. Prepaid fees became unsecured claims and one service had to buy new laptops. The displaced migrated cloud to cloud — two vendors to replace one.

Finding 07

The obvious product is the wrong one

Per-vehicle dispatch SaaS against Ontario's addressable fleet tops out near $4–6M ARR at total penetration. The value is not in selling consoles to operators — it is in owning the allocation decision and the settlement rail.

03

Five silos, no system

Ontario already proved it can coordinate patient transport — for the acute, high-severity, provincially-funded minority. CritiCall's Repatriation Tool has served every acute hospital since 2014, and Ornge moved 23,725 patient legs in 2024/25 across a million square kilometres.

The routine 80% never got that treatment, because no ministry owns it.

The regulatory hole

Non-urgent patient transfer sits outside the Ambulance Act: no licensing, no vehicle standard, no crew standard, no price ceiling. A coroner has called for regulation since 1995; a 2011 bill died when the legislature dissolved; Ontario's Ombudsman has called paramedic oversight "complicated, overburdened, and wholly inadequate."

Twenty-five years of failed attempts say do not bet on regulation arriving. Build a credentialing and audit spine anyway — so that if it lands, you are the reference implementation rather than a casualty.

04

Who pays — the crux

Emergency transport has one funding structure and a regulated price. Everything else has neither.

Emergency (911)

Regulated · ~$2B/yr

  • Province 50% / municipality 50%
  • Patient co-payment $45
  • $240 if deemed not medically necessary
  • Statutory exemptions exist — though "many people are not aware" of them

Non-urgent transport

Unregulated · no OHIP coverage

  • Hospital budget · LTC home budget
  • Patient and family, out of pocket, no exemption
  • NIHB — $166.0M in Ontario (2023–24)
  • Northern Health Travel Grant — reimbursed after the fact
  • WSIB · private insurers · charities
  • Ontario Health atHome and community agencies + co-pay
  • Municipal paratransit + fare

Three properties make this the business model, not just context

"Medically necessary" is undefined in policy, legislation or regulation. Hospitals have told the Patient Ombudsman that needing a stretcher does not by itself establish medical necessity. The most consequential financial decision in the trip has no decision support behind it.

Nobody sees total cost. One patient can consume a municipal paratransit trip on Monday, a hospital-funded transfer on Wednesday and an NIHB-funded trip on Friday. No system reconciles this.

Eligibility goes unclaimed. The Northern grant requires the patient to front the money and file afterward — roughly 200,000 reimbursements processed in 2023–24, at $0.41/km. Automated eligibility and split settlement is genuinely unbuilt infrastructure.

The most valuable regulatory fact here

Ontario's OADS v4.0 — the Ambulance Documentation Standards, mandatory since 2 September 2025 under O. Reg. 257/00 — is the compliance moat that protects the incumbent ePCR vendors. It binds Ambulance Service Operators, paramedics and Base Hospitals.

Because non-urgent patient transfer sits outside the Ambulance Act, it also sits outside OADS. A transport product that never becomes the 911 record of care does not have to cross that moat. The same absence of regulation that makes this market chaotic to operate in makes it cheap to enter with software.

⚠️ The exemption is conditional. Right-sizing triage decides whether a patient needs an ambulance — the nearer that moves to diverting emergency resources, the nearer it moves back to the regulated boundary. Display and record clinician decisions; never derive them. And confirm the exemption with a legal opinion before it carries weight in a pitch.

05

Demand is compounding

Three forces push the same direction, and one of them is being deliberately created by provincial policy.

Demographics

Ontario had 3.1 million people aged 65+ in 2025 — 18.9% of the population, heading to 4.6 million by 2051, with every baby boomer past 65 by 2031. That cohort has grown ~1.7× since the transfer study was published, against ~1.25× total population growth.

Recurring treatment travel

More than 11,000 Ontarians are on dialysis, roughly 78% of them in-centre. In-centre means three trips a week, indefinitely — no other patient group in health care carries that transport dependency. It generates about 1.33 million round trips a year, every one of them known in advance.

Policy is manufacturing trips

Ontario funded 57 new community surgical and diagnostic centres at $155M over two years, including 35 for MRI and CT serving a claimed 800,000+ patients. Every one of them decouples a procedure from a hospital — and creates a journey that did not previously exist.

The whitespace, stated plainly

Nobody occupies the intersection of clinical acuity triage × multi-provider neutrality × multi-payer settlement × Ontario regulatory fit.

US SaaS has neutrality and no clinical or payer fit. Uber Health has scale and no acuity. Operator portals have clinical fit and no neutrality. Transit tech has optimisation and no clinical model. CritiCall has coordination and no reach into the routine 80%.

Ranked by defensibility against evidence of pain.

S1

Acuity triage & right-sizing

Highest value

Standing orders for dialysis, oncology, rehab and wound care; pooling across patients and providers; automatic re-optimisation on schedule change.

S3

Neutral booking exchange

One PHIPA-safe request, fanned to a multi-provider market with real acceptance and ETA. Directly contests operator-captive portals — and has the shortest window before consolidation closes it.

Time-limited
S4

Discharge-flow integration

Predict discharge readiness from hospital signals, pre-book transport, tie vehicle arrival to bed release. Sells against ALC days, which hospitals already measure.

Near-term
S5

Eligibility & split settlement

Determine who pays which fraction — NIHB, the Northern grant, hospital, LTC, insurer, patient — and settle it. Unbuilt. The deepest moat and the slowest build.

S6

Northern & Indigenous journeys

Multi-leg land and air, grant pre-authorisation instead of after-the-fact reimbursement, Ornge and Hope Air handoffs. A federal payer growing 20% a year, and the strongest equity case.

Single payer
S7

Quality & credentialing spine

Crew credentials, vehicle standards, incident reporting and outcome tracking in a sector with none. Low near-term revenue; the highest-leverage asset if regulation ever lands.

The rule this implies

Price against avoided cost, never against seats. Every viable model here requires sitting in either the allocation decision or the flow of funds. A product that sits in neither is a $4M business — and does not justify the clinical and regulatory work this domain demands.

What carries over from EMSS

Corrected. The first version of this section assumed EMSS meant a dispatch/CAD system and listed dispatch logic, AVL and crew scheduling as assets that carried over. The codebase has now been read. None of those exist, and every row of the original table was wrong.

EMSS is AidPost — an offline-first PWA for casualty care and transport documentation. ~45,500 lines across 195 files. Responders capture identity, injuries on an anatomical body chart, vitals and treatments, then hand over to a hospital as an HL7 FHIR R4 bundle. Multilingual with right-to-left support.

The thesis does not transfer — and that is the important part

AidPost's differentiator, sharpened across four survey passes, is "no backend, no sync server, no vendor relationship." Its own fourth pass retired "works offline" as a headline because the incumbents do offline capture too; what remains distinctive is the absence of a server, an account and a vendor.

A transport coordination layer cannot be built that way. Matching a request to a provider, settling across payers and holding shared fleet state are inherently multi-party and networked. A booking exchange requires a server by definition — precisely the shape AidPost defines itself against.

Reuse the engine. Do not reuse the pitch. Dispatch, ETA prediction, pooling, fleet management, booking, eligibility and settlement are all new work. That is most of the product.

AidPost's own commercialization file scores Official EMS at ★ fit, ★ accessibility, heavy regulatory load, 12–24+ months — "the biggest prize but a fortress" — and recommends the humanitarian path instead. A patient transportation product is not that fortress: outside the Ambulance Act, outside OADS, logistics rather than SaMD. But it is still Ontario health-system selling, and it is a second front for the same team. Those are resourcing decisions, not research findings.

10

Recommendation

Do not build a dispatch platform. Build the allocation and settlement layer Ontario does not have — starting from a single wedge with a real budget holder and a measurable outcome.

Recommended beachhead

Recurring dialysis transport, sold to regional renal programs

  1. Frequency. Three trips a week per patient, indefinitely. ~11,000 dialysis patients, ~78% in-centre — about 1.33 million round trips a year.
  2. Schedulability. Treatment slots are fixed and known in advance. This is the most poolable demand in the entire health system.
  3. An identified buyer. Regional renal programs under Ontario Health, with an existing performance-reporting relationship.
  4. A metric already tracked. Missed treatments — 10% monthly, 35% quarterly — with published morbidity consequences. Value provable in one quarter.
  5. A natural expansion path. Oncology and rehab share the recurring structure, and dialysis patients also generate discharge and inter-facility trips — walking you into S4 and S3 through a door you already hold.
S2 Dialysis→S4 Discharge flow→S3 Neutral exchange→S6 Northern & Indigenous→S5 Settlement

One caution from the EMSS survey

The Prehos collapse is the closest thing this market has to a natural experiment, and it cuts against architectural pitches. Twenty-two Ontario services were displaced by a commercial failure — not an outage — and the one documented migration went cloud to cloud, needing two vendors to replace one.

Sell the outcome, not the architecture. For the dialysis wedge that means missed treatments avoided — a number the buyer already tracks and already answers for. Data ownership and continuity are a strong second argument and a poor first one.

Two decisions the research cannot make

Whether this product is a coordination layer, an operator, or a payer-side utility — and which wedge gets funded first. Both are the team's calls. Everything above is the input to them.

Method and its limits. Research conducted August 2026. Direct document retrieval was blocked by network egress policy for every primary-source domain attempted — including ICES, PubMed Central, ontario.ca, CIHI and the Ombudsman. That is adequate for shaping product strategy. It is not adequate for a business case, a board deck, or anything a third party will rely on.
